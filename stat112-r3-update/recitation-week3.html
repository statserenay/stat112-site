Part A — Histograms in depth

NoteKey terms: histograms
  • Class (bin): an interval of values. Class width = upper limit − lower limit. A first guess is \(\dfrac{\text{largest} - \text{smallest}}{\text{number of classes}}\), rounded up to a convenient number.
  • Number of classes: more data allows more classes. Too few classes hide the shape; too many turn the histogram into noise.
  • Class midpoint = (lower limit + upper limit) / 2. It represents all values in the class.
  • Relative frequency = class frequency / \(n\). Use it to compare groups of different sizes.
  • Density = relative frequency / class width. Use it for the bar heights when classes have unequal widths, so that the area of each bar matches the share of the data.
  • Modal class: the class with the highest frequency. A histogram with two clear peaks is bimodal, which often means two different groups are mixed together.
  • Unlike a bar chart, the bars of a histogram touch: the classes cover a continuous number line.

  • \(n = 6 + 14 + 24 + 18 + 10 + 5 + 3 = 80\), so the relative frequency of 30–45 is \(24/80 = 0.30\). It is also the modal class (24 is the largest frequency).
  • Midpoint of 45–60: \((45 + 60)/2 = 52.5\).
  • 60 minutes or more: \((10 + 5 + 3)/80 = 18/80 = 22.5\%\).
  • Below 45: \((6 + 14 + 24)/80 = 44/80 = 55\%\). The 40th and 41st ordered values lie between the 20th and the 44th observation, so the median class is 30–45.
  • Grouped mean: each class is represented by its midpoint: \[\bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum f_i m_i}{n} = \frac{6(7.5) + 14(22.5) + 24(37.5) + 18(52.5) + 10(67.5) + 5(82.5) + 3(97.5)}{80} = \frac{3585}{80} = 44.81\] This is only an estimate, because the raw data are lost once they are grouped.

Which statements about building a histogram are true? Select all that apply.

  • With too few classes, important features such as a second peak can disappear.
  • With too many classes for a small data set, the histogram becomes spiky and hard to read.
  • A first guess for the class width is (largest − smallest) / (number of classes), rounded up to a convenient number.
  • Changing the class width changes the data, so only one histogram of a data set is correct.
  • The first class should start at, or a little below, the smallest observation.
  • Like a bar chart, a histogram should have gaps between its bars.
  • d is false: the data stay the same; only the picture changes. That is why it is good practice to try a few widths.
  • f is false: the bars of a histogram touch, because the classes cover a continuous scale. Gaps belong to bar charts of categories.

Describe the shape of each histogram.

  • Histogram A :: Negatively skewed
  • Histogram B :: Bimodal
  • Histogram C :: Uniform
  • Histogram D :: Positively skewed
  • Histogram E :: Symmetric
  • A: long tail to the left → negatively skewed (mean < median).
  • B: two clear peaks → bimodal. Ask whether two groups are mixed.
  • C: all bars about the same height → uniform (flat).
  • D: long tail to the right → positively skewed (mean > median).
  • E: mound-shaped and mirror-symmetric → symmetric. This is the shape where the empirical rule works.

600 customers’ spending at a campus café (in ₺) is shown with three different bin widths.

Which histogram reveals that most customers spend a round amount (a multiple of 5 ₺)?

  • H1 (bin width 40 ₺)
  • H2 (bin width 10 ₺)
  • H3 (bin width 1 ₺)
  • None of them: a histogram can never show this

Only the narrow bins of H3 show the tall spikes at 50, 55, 60, … ₺: many receipts are rounded to a multiple of 5 (this is called heaping). With wide bins (H1, H2) the spikes are averaged away and the distribution simply looks right-skewed.

Lesson: wide bins show the overall shape; narrow bins show fine details of how the data were recorded. Look at both before you interpret.

The ages of 110 students in an evening course were grouped into classes of unequal width:

Age 18–20 20–22 22–30
Frequency 30 40 40

If you draw the bars with heights equal to the frequencies, the class 22–30 looks as “popular” as 20–22, even though it is four times as wide. Instead, use frequency per year of age (frequency / class width) as the height:

18–20: students per year, 20–22: students per year, 22–30: students per year.

Class widths are 2, 2 and 8 years: \(30/2 = 15\), \(40/2 = 20\) and \(40/8 = 5\).

With these heights, the area of each bar (height × width) equals its frequency (15 × 2 = 30, 20 × 2 = 40, 5 × 8 = 40), so the picture is honest: students aged 22–30 are spread thinly over many ages. (Dividing again by \(n\) gives the density, whose total area is 1.) Open-ended classes such as “30 and over” have no width at all, which is why they can’t be drawn fairly.

Section A has 40 students and Section B has 160 students. You want to compare the shape of their exam score distributions with two histograms side by side. What should the vertical axis show?

  • Frequencies, because they are the real counts
  • Relative frequencies (or percentages)
  • Cumulative frequencies
  • It does not matter, the comparison is the same either way

With frequencies, Section B’s bars are about four times as tall just because it is bigger, which hides the comparison of shapes. Relative frequencies put both histograms on the same scale (each sums to 1). Also use the same classes on both histograms.

The heights of all members of a university sports club form a bimodal histogram, with one peak near 168 cm and another near 192 cm. The club has a gymnastics team and a basketball team.

Explain what the two peaks probably mean, and suggest a better way to display the data.

Model answer: The two peaks suggest that two different groups are mixed together: gymnasts (shorter) and basketball players (taller). One overall mean (around 180 cm) would describe nobody well. A better display separates the groups: two histograms with the same classes (relative frequencies), or side-by-side box plots or violin plots by team.

Put the steps for constructing a histogram by hand in the correct order.

  • Find the smallest and largest observations and the range
  • Choose the number of classes (more data → more classes)
  • Compute the class width and round it up to a convenient number
  • Set the class limits, starting at or slightly below the smallest value
  • Count the frequency (and relative frequency) of each class
  • Draw adjoining bars whose heights are the frequencies (or relative frequencies)

You need the range before you can choose a width, and the class limits before you can count. If the class widths are unequal, use density for the bar heights in the last step.

Part B — Frequency polygons, dot plots and outliers

Look at the frequency polygon in the explanation of the first question (phone time before sleeping, classes of width 15 from 0 to 105 minutes).

Each point of a frequency polygon is plotted at the class . The first point of the data is at \(x =\) . To close the polygon, we add one extra point with frequency 0 at each end, at \(x =\) and \(x =\) .

The points are the midpoints 7.5, 22.5, …, 97.5 with heights equal to the frequencies. The extra points sit one class width (15) beyond the first and last midpoints: \(7.5 - 15 = -7.5\) and \(97.5 + 15 = 112.5\).

Frequency polygons are handy for comparing two distributions on one graph, because two lines overlap more readably than two sets of bars.

An outlier is a value that is unusually large or small compared with the rest. Before you remove one, find out why it is there. What is the most likely explanation in each case?

  • A student’s height is recorded as 1.78 cm :: Recording or data-entry error
  • A phone in the battery test above lasts 22 hours; it turns out to be the newer “Pro” model with a bigger battery :: Comes from a different population
  • In a study of adults’ blood pressure, one participant is a 9-year-old child :: Comes from a different population
  • A “daily screen time” of 31 hours :: Recording or data-entry error
  • The highest river level in 50 years of daily measurements, recorded during a once-in-a-century flood :: Correct, but a rare event
  • A penthouse that rents for ₺29,450 a month in a data set of student flats :: Correct, but a rare event | Comes from a different population
  • Errors (1.78 cm, 31 hours a day) should be corrected if possible, or removed.
  • Different population (another phone model, a child among adults): exclude it, but say so in your report.
  • Rare but real values (a record flood) must stay: they are often the most important observations! Report results with and without them if they change the conclusions.
  • The penthouse can be argued either way: it is a real rent, but a luxury flat is arguably not part of the “student flat” market.

Part C — Scatter plots and correlation

NoteKey terms: two numerical variables
  • Explanatory variable (\(x\), horizontal axis): the one that may explain or predict changes in the other. Response variable (\(y\), vertical axis): the outcome.
  • Describe a scatter plot by its form (linear or curved), direction (positive or negative), strength (how closely the points follow the pattern) and any unusual points.
  • Pearson correlation coefficient: \[r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}\,S_{yy}}}, \qquad S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}),\quad S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2,\quad S_{yy} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\]
  • \(-1 \le r \le 1\). The sign gives the direction, \(|r|\) the strength of the linear relationship. \(r\) has no units, and it does not change if you swap \(x\) and \(y\) or change units (cm → inches).
  • \(r\) measures only linear association, and it is sensitive to outliers.

For each pair, which variable is the explanatory variable and which is the response?

  • Hours of sunlight per day (in a study of plant growth) :: Explanatory
  • Plant growth (cm per week) :: Response
  • Price of a cup of coffee (in a study of daily sales) :: Explanatory
  • Number of cups of coffee sold per day :: Response
  • Daily maximum temperature (in a study of electricity use) :: Explanatory
  • Daily electricity use for air conditioning :: Response

The explanatory variable is the one we change or that comes first and could influence the other: sunlight → growth, price → sales, temperature → electricity use. By convention it goes on the horizontal axis. If there is no natural direction (e.g. height of siblings), either variable can go on either axis.

Match each scatter plot with the best description.

  • P1 :: Strong positive (r ≈ 0.9)
  • P2 :: Strong negative (r ≈ −0.9)
  • P3 :: Moderate positive (r ≈ 0.5)
  • P4 :: No relationship (r ≈ 0)
  • P5 :: Moderate negative (r ≈ −0.5)
  • P6 :: Strong curved pattern, yet r ≈ 0

The computed values are P1: 0.91, P2: −0.87, P3: 0.54, P4: 0.00, P5: −0.48 and P6: −0.01.

P6 is the trap: the points follow a perfect U-shape, so \(y\) is almost completely determined by \(x\), yet \(r \approx 0\). The rising and falling halves cancel out. \(r = 0\) means no linear relationship, not no relationship. Always plot the data before trusting \(r\).

Which statements about the correlation coefficient \(r\) are true? Select all that apply.

  • \(r\) has no units.
  • Measuring height in inches instead of centimetres does not change \(r\).
  • \(r = 0\) means there is no linear relationship.
  • A single extreme point can change \(r\) a lot.
  • If \(r = 0.8\), then 80% of the points lie on a straight line.
  • \(r\) is always between −1 and 1.
  • Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) changes the sign of \(r\).
  • e is false: \(r\) is not a percentage of points; it measures how tightly the cloud follows a line.
  • g is false: the formula is symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\), so swapping them gives exactly the same \(r\).

Without student Z, \(r = 0.09\). With student Z, \(r = 0.86\). Which conclusion is best?

  • There is clearly a strong linear relationship between tutoring and scores, since \(r = 0.86\).
  • Student Z is an outlier, so we should delete Z and report \(r = 0.09\) without comment.
  • The strong correlation is created by a single point; investigate Z and report the results both with and without Z.
  • \(r\) cannot be computed when there is an outlier.

Among the twelve typical students there is practically no linear relationship (\(r = 0.09\)). One extreme student turns it into \(r = 0.86\): such a point is called influential. Neither blindly trusting 0.86 nor silently deleting Z is honest. Find out who Z is (a data error? a different kind of student?) and show both analyses.

The data show 140 students, but the left plot seems to contain only about 15 dots. Why, and what does jittering do?

  • Most students did not answer, so their dots are missing.
  • Many students have exactly the same (courses, sleep) pair, so their dots are drawn on top of each other. Jittering adds a little random noise to the plotted positions so that overlapping points become visible.
  • Jittering removes outliers so that the plot looks cleaner.
  • Jittering rounds the data so that more points fall on the same spot.

Both variables are discrete (whole numbers of courses and rounded hours), so dozens of students share each position: this is overplotting. Jittering moves each dot by a tiny random amount (here at most ±0.25) only in the picture. Transparency (semi-see-through dots) helps too, especially with thousands of points.

After jittering the plot above, we should compute the correlation from the jittered values, because they are more accurate.

False. The random noise is only a display trick. All statistics (\(\bar{x}\), \(s\), \(r\), …) must be computed from the original data.

Part D — More graphs for numerical data

Name each graph.

  • G1 :: Lollipop chart
  • G2 :: Slope chart
  • G3 :: Dumbbell plot
  • G4 :: Line chart (time series)
  • G5 :: Frequency polygon
  • G6 :: Bubble chart
  • G1, lollipop: a bar chart in which each bar is replaced by a thin line with a dot. Less ink, same information.
  • G2, slope chart: each line connects an item’s values at two time points; the slope shows the change, and crossing lines show rank changes.
  • G3, dumbbell: two dots per category (two years, two groups) joined by a bar that shows the gap.
  • G4, line chart: values over time, connected in time order.
  • G5, frequency polygon: frequencies at class midpoints, joined by straight lines.
  • G6, bubble chart: a scatter plot whose bubble size shows a third numerical variable.

Match each graph with the job it does best.

  • Lollipop chart :: Compare values across categories, like a bar chart but with less ink
  • Slope chart :: Show how each item changed between two time points, including changes in rank
  • Dumbbell plot :: Show the gap between two values (two years or two groups) for each category
  • Bubble chart :: Show three numerical variables in one plot
  • Scatter plot matrix :: Look at every pairwise relationship among several numerical variables
  • Violin plot :: Compare the shape of distributions across groups, including multiple peaks
  • Line chart :: Show how a variable changes over time

Choosing the graph starts with the question: compare categories (lollipop, bar), show change (slope, dumbbell, line), show relationships (scatter, bubble, scatter matrix) or show distributions (histogram, box, violin).

Both sections have \(Q_1 = 52\), median \(= 60\) and \(Q_3 = 68\). What does the violin plot show that the box plot hides?

  • Section L has a higher median than Section K.
  • Section L has more outliers than Section K.
  • Section K is bimodal.
  • Section L is bimodal: its scores form two separate groups, around 50 and around 70, with few students near the median.

The boxes are identical because they use only five numbers. The violin adds the density (a smoothed histogram), mirrored on both sides, and reveals that Section L consists of two groups and almost nobody scores near 60. Perhaps the section contains students who did and did not take the preparatory course. A box plot can never show multiple peaks.

Are these time-series or cross-sectional data?

  • Turkey’s monthly inflation rate from 2015 to 2026 :: Time series
  • The GPAs of all STAT 112 students this semester :: Cross-sectional
  • The daily number of visitors to the METU library during October :: Time series
  • The rents of 160 flats near METU in September 2026 :: Cross-sectional
  • Your weight, measured every Monday for a year :: Time series
  • The heights of the players of the national basketball team in 2026 :: Cross-sectional

Time-series data follow one unit (a country, a library, a person) through successive points in time; the order of the observations matters, and a line chart is the natural graph. Cross-sectional data measure many units at one point in time; their order does not matter.

Figure F6 shows the average asking rent of flats near METU each month, January 2025 to September 2026. Describe the two main patterns you see and suggest an explanation for each.

Model answer: (1) A clear upward trend: the average asking rent rose from about ₺13,000 to about ₺22,000 over 21 months, mostly because of inflation. This is when new and returning students are looking for flats before the semester starts, so demand is highest. A line chart is the right graph here because these are time-series data: the order of the months matters.
